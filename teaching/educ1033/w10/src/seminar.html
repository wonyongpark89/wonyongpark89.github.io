Recap: health, health promotion and health literacy

Health is broad

Physical, mental and social well-being, not merely the absence of disease (WHO, 1946).

Information is not enough

The Ottawa Charter sets out five areas of action. Developing personal skills, where education sits, is only one (WHO, 1986).

Three forms of health literacy

Functional: using basic information. Interactive: asking and seeking support. Critical: evaluating claims (Nutbeam, 2000).

WHO (1946), Constitution; WHO (1986), Ottawa Charter; Nutbeam (2000), Health Promotion International 15(3).

A quick reminder of Part 1 of the lecture. In England, RSHE now sets out what pupils should learn about health and safety, from handwashing and road safety in primary to evaluating online information and CPR in secondary.

Recap: knowing is not the same as doing

C

Capability: knowledge and practical skills.

O

Opportunity: time, money, places and equipment; friends, family and norms.

M

Motivation: intentions, and automatic processes such as habit and enjoyment.

LifeLab and EACH-B: pupils valued the LifeLab day, but diet and physical activity did not measurably change. Lessons build capability; opportunity and motivation lie largely outside them.

Michie, van Stralen & West (2011), Implementation Science 6:42, Figure 1, p. 4. CC BY 2.0; Barker et al. (2026); Jenner et al. (2025).

COM-B is the tool for Activity 1. Remind students of the meal example: knowing how to cook (capability), access to ingredients and a kitchen (opportunity), enjoyment and habit (motivation).

Recap: school safety is more than a lesson

Safety means managing risk

A hazard is a source of harm; risk depends on circumstances. Safety means significant risks are managed, not that harm is impossible.

Change the setting first

The hierarchy of controls puts elimination, substitution and engineering before procedures and protective equipment.

Three pillars

Safer facilities; safety and continuity management; risk reduction and resilience education, on a foundation of systems and policies.

UNDRR (2017); DfE (2022), section 5; GADRRRES (2022), pp. 1–3.

These three ideas set up Activity 2. Pillar 2 includes educational continuity: whether every pupil can keep learning when hazards disrupt school.

Scenario: healthy eating in a Year 9 tutor group

Pupils have had lessons on balanced diets and can explain them. Here is what six of them say about what they actually eat.

Maya

We learned about balanced meals, but I've never actually cooked one.

Josh

The lunch queue takes most of the break, so I grab something on the way in.

Aisha

My parents work late, and there's not much in to cook with.

Leo

Everyone in my group gets the same snack after school. It's just what we do.

Priya

I know fruit is better for me. I just don't fancy it.

Sam

The healthier options at the shop near us cost more.

A hypothetical scenario, built from the barriers described in Michie et al. (2011) and the Ottawa Charter. Read the statements aloud or give groups a minute to read. Several statements fit more than one COM-B component, which is the point: barriers interact. The answer key follows the activity.

Several statements fit more than one component: barriers interact, so accept other answers that are well argued. Josh, Aisha and Sam all describe physical opportunity: time, food at home and cost. None of these is solved by knowing more. Leo shows social opportunity and automatic motivation working together. Priya shows that knowing is not the same as wanting. The school-side changes do not rely on pupils trying harder; lessons build capability, changing the setting builds opportunity, and the Ottawa Charter asks for both. Avoid assuming families are indifferent: Aisha’s family may lack time or money rather than interest.

Scenario: a school facing repeated hot days

The school

  • A secondary school has several very hot days each summer term
  • Top-floor classrooms overheat; the playing field has little shade
  • Summer exams take place in the sports hall
  • Some pupils travel long distances on foot or by bus
  • Letters home go out by one app, in English

From the sources

  • In April 2024, 123 million students worldwide had schooling disrupted, mostly by heatwaves (UNICEF, 2025)
  • Recurring overheating may need changes to buildings and routines, not only advice (GADRRRES, 2022)
  • Continuity plans should ask which pupils face extra barriers and how families receive information

Hypothetical scenario. UNICEF (2025), Figure 1, p. 3; GADRRRES (2022).

The scenario details are invented for the activity; the right-hand column comes from the week's sources. Draw attention to the last two school details: travel and communication are where equity questions usually appear.

Start the 15-minute timer (play button or T); it gives a soft chime at the end. Then about 5 minutes of feedback: ask each group for one action per pillar and build a shared list. Possible answers. Pillar 1: shade, blinds, ventilation, water points, moving lessons or exams out of the hottest rooms. Pillar 2: a hot-weather plan with trigger points, adjusted timetables and uniform rules, who decides and how families are told (several channels and languages). Pillar 3: lessons on heat and wellbeing, recognising symptoms, how to ask for help. Follow-up questions if time allows: which pupils face extra barriers (health conditions, disabilities, long journeys, families who do not use the app or read English), and which actions change the setting rather than relying on behaviour (water points and shade versus advice to drink water).
